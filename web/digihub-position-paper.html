A strategic position for bioregional digital sovereignty — from extraction to circulation, from dependency to collective capability.

DigiHub Liechtenstein · digihub.li · EDIH Network · February 2026
In One Minute

Digital sovereignty, when pursued in isolation, produces fragmentation. When embedded within a regenerative economic paradigm and enacted through commons-based institutional innovation, it becomes the foundation for a fundamentally different kind of digital ecosystem — one that builds collective capability rather than extracting individual value. The DigiHub Liechtenstein, operating within the European Digital Innovation Hub network, is prototyping this approach at bioregional scale. Through Programmable Organizational Domains, commons-based infrastructure, and collaborative finance mechanisms, it demonstrates that small, agile jurisdictions can serve as laboratories for institutional patterns that are globally replicable. The question is not whether to pursue digital sovereignty, but what kind of economy sovereignty serves.

Section I

Why — The Sovereignty-Regeneration Nexus

There is a moment in every dependency crisis when an organization realizes it has been building on someone else's land. The cloud provider changes terms. The software vendor pivots. The critical service gets acquired. Choices made in procurement meetings years ago become existential threats.

This is the sovereignty moment — when abstraction becomes concrete, when "strategic autonomy" shifts from policy document to operational imperative. Across Europe, municipalities, SMEs, and public institutions are confronting a landscape of digital dependencies that was assembled incrementally but now constitutes a structural vulnerability.

Yet most conversations about digital sovereignty happen, as Simon Wardley observes, without maps. They are storytelling exercises disconnected from the actual landscape of dependencies and evolution — equivalent to generals trying to win a war by giving it the right name. Without situational awareness of what we depend on, how those dependencies are evolving, and where our leverage actually lies, sovereignty remains an aspiration rather than a strategy.

The deeper problem is that sovereignty, even when pursued with maps and strategic clarity, requires an economic paradigm to inhabit. Sovereignty from extractive platforms is necessary — but sovereignty for what? Without answering this question, digital sovereignty risks becoming a protectionist reflex rather than a generative project.

Sovereignty is fundamentally different from independence. It is inherently relational, not isolationist. Medieval guilds understood something we have forgotten: mastery compounds through community, not competition. A guild member was sovereign not because they worked alone, but because they belonged to a network of mutual obligation, shared standards, and collective capability that no single patron could destroy.

The Dutch government's digital stack model and Ton Zijlstra's framework reveal that the strategic question is not "do everything yourself" — it is a three-way choice:

  • Self — Layers core to organizational identity where dependency creates existential risk.
  • Together — Layers with shared requirements, where collective capability exceeds individual capacity.
  • Others — Layers sufficiently commoditized where vendor competition keeps quality high.

The sovereignty movement's purpose is expanding the "Together" category — building collective capability where dependency creates vulnerability but individual action is inefficient.

Igor Calzada's research identifies three overlapping layers through which sovereignty can be enacted: institutional sovereignty, embedded in public agencies and organizations; cooperative sovereignty, realized through federated data cooperatives and trusts; and geopolitical sovereignty, enacted through interregional alliances and standards. This multi-layered framing moves beyond the binary of "sovereign or dependent" toward a nuanced architecture of graduated autonomy.

We therefore prefer the framing of digital self-determination — the capacity of individuals, organizations, and communities to make meaningful choices about their digital infrastructure, data, and economic participation. Self-determination implies agency within relationship, not isolation.

Sustainability asks: how do we do less damage? Regeneration asks: how do we design systems that become healthier through use? This distinction matters for digital infrastructure because it reframes the goal from mitigating harm (data protection, reducing lock-in) to designing for systemic vitality (building collective capability, creating commons dividends).

Three intellectual traditions converge to form the regenerative digital ecosystem paradigm:

Doughnut Economics as normative compass

Kate Raworth's framework establishes the design space: a social foundation of human needs below which no one should fall, and an ecological ceiling above which planetary boundaries are breached. A regenerative digital ecosystem operates within this doughnut — ensuring digital infrastructure serves social foundations while respecting ecological limits.

Commons governance as institutional logic

Elinor Ostrom's research demonstrated that communities can govern shared resources sustainably when institutional arrangements match the resource's characteristics. Her design principles — clearly defined boundaries, collective-choice arrangements, monitoring, graduated sanctions, conflict-resolution mechanisms, nested governance — provide the institutional blueprint for governing digital commons.

Bioregionalism as operational scale

Bioregional self-sufficiency and self-determination necessitate rebuilding production capacities, upscaling resource coordination, and establishing bioregional institutions and currencies. The bioregion — defined by ecological and cultural boundaries rather than administrative ones — provides the operational scale at which regenerative digital ecosystems become tangible.

The convergence: sovereignty + regeneration + commons = Regenerative Digital Ecosystem. A bioregional digital infrastructure and institutional arrangement designed to build collective capability, governed as commons, operating within ecological and social boundaries.

The rise of generative AI has accelerated this awareness — as Calzada warns, GenAI exacerbates concentration and asymmetry, creating "data-opolies" that undermine democratic accountability and trust.

Section II

What — The Regenerative Digital Ecosystem Model

Core Diagram
The Regenerative Digital Ecosystem — three-layer architecture

The model operates across three interdependent layers:

  • Institutional Layer — Programmable Organizational Domains (PODs) as the organizational primitives, governed through commons principles, nested from individual to bioregional scale.
  • Infrastructure Layer — Decentralized identifiers, end-to-end encrypted collaboration spaces, AI oracle services, and interoperable protocols — civic protocol infrastructure, not platform lock-in.
  • Economic Layer — Collaborative finance mechanisms, tokenized coordination, outcome-based funding, and commons dividends — economic patterns that circulate rather than extract value.

[To be expanded: how the three layers interact, the design principles governing each, the relationship to the Self/Together/Others framework]

A Programmable Organizational Domain is not a product — it is an institutional primitive. A POD enables any entity to establish a sovereign digital presence, manage its data and economic participation, and connect to a commons-based ecosystem — all governed by protocols rather than platforms.

The core value proposition: "Have a digital twin of your organization. We provide secure data, local AI." But this framing, while useful for adoption, understates the structural significance. PODs represent a new category of institutional form — modular, composable, and governed by their participants.

The technical infrastructure of a regenerative digital ecosystem must itself embody commons principles: open protocols, interoperability, resistance to platform capture, and governance by participants.

The DigiHub operates on a stack built around several core components: decentralized identifiers (DIDs) providing self-sovereign identity; Matrix-based end-to-end encrypted collaboration spaces; AI "oracle services" that organizations can deploy, own, and monetize; and protocol-driven verification and credentialing systems.

Critically, this infrastructure is designed as a civic protocol — not a platform. The distinction matters: platforms extract value by becoming intermediaries; protocols enable value by becoming standards. The oracle marketplace model, where 80% of value flows to the service provider and the platform takes only a coordination fee, embodies this principle.

The economic layer of the regenerative digital ecosystem draws on several convergent traditions that share a common insight: the dominant monetary monoculture is a root cause of both economic fragility and local economy impoverishment.

Collaborative Finance & Inside Money

The Collaborative Finance sphere, as articulated by Paolo Dini, builds on endogenous money theory and emphasizes strengthening "inside money" circuits through mutual credit systems, credit obligation schemes, and network clearing algorithms. This is not abstract theory — it is the operational logic of how bioregional economies can achieve monetary self-determination alongside digital self-determination.

The Commons Dividend

In the venture capital model, successful exits return capital to investors who deploy it in new startups. Value concentrates in fund managers. In the sovereignty model, successful transformations return capability to the commons, making future transformations easier. The first cohort pays the full cost of developing procurement templates, security frameworks, and migration playbooks. The second pays only adaptation costs. The tenth pays near zero — sovereignty becomes standard practice.

This is the Commons Dividend: shared infrastructure experiencing falling per-unit costs as membership grows, creating living knowledge that improves through use, maintained by a community that directly benefits from its quality.

Civic Protocol Economies

The Civic Protocol Economy framework explores innovations at the intersection of Civic Economies (relational, wellbeing-oriented) and Protocol Economies (enabled by smart contracts, DIDs, automated governance). A Civic Protocol Economy consists of relational economic systems, governed by their participants, represented through digital identities, that account for externalities, and are partially managed by digital agents.

Section III

How — Liechtenstein as Prototype

Liechtenstein is not merely a convenient location for this work — it is a structurally privileged laboratory. Several characteristics converge to create a unique conditions for prototyping regenerative digital ecosystems:

  • Governance agility — As a micro-state, the distance between policy insight and policy implementation is extraordinarily short. The TVTG (Token and VT Service Provider Act), commonly known as the Blockchain Act, demonstrates this agility.
  • Legal innovation — The Liechtenstein Venture Cooperative (LVC) legal form, under which DigiHub itself operates, provides a native vehicle for commons-based economic coordination.
  • Digital identity readiness — E-ID infrastructure and progressive data protection laws align with the DID-based architecture of the ecosystem.
  • Bioregional embeddedness — Situated in the Alpine region with deep connections to the DACH economies and EU/EFTA frameworks, Liechtenstein operates at the intersection of bioregional rootedness and international connectivity.
  • EDIH mandate — As part of the European Digital Innovation Hub network, DigiHub Liechtenstein has an institutional mandate and network to translate local prototypes into European patterns.

The DigiHub's stated mission captures the ambition: "We make Liechtenstein a lighthouse for positive digital transformation that connects humans through trust, transparency, and purpose-driven action."

Awareness & Credibility

Organizations confront dependency vulnerability. Early movement members provide peer validation: "We faced the same crisis. Here's our journey." The sovereignty movement lends operational credibility — not financial, but experiential.

Coalition Formation

Organizations join communities of practice using common assessment frameworks. In isolation, each procurement budget is too small to influence vendors. Together, they represent a market that cannot be ignored. Procurement frameworks become shared standards.

3
Year 3–5
Capability Building

Organizations develop expertise in different domains. Each contributes mastery to the commons while drawing on others' expertise. The cost of sovereignty drops dramatically when infrastructure is shared. This stage requires deep investment in organizational change — building sovereignty is cultural, not just technical.

4
Year 5–8
Strategic Autonomy

Organizations achieve meaningful independence. They can credibly negotiate. Legacy vendors must adapt or lose market share. The power dynamic inverts from supplication to negotiation among peers.

5
Year 8+
Normalization

Sovereignty disappears into normalcy. It is no longer a "project" — it is simply how things are done. Embedded in procurement policy, hiring practices, organizational culture. The ultimate success: when strategic autonomy becomes unremarkable.

DigiHub Liechtenstein currently operates at the boundary of Stages 1 and 2 — building awareness while simultaneously forming the coalitions and infrastructure needed for Stage 3.

Analytical Frameworks Used

Wardley Mapping, developed by Simon Wardley, plots value chains across an axis of evolution — from genesis through custom-built and product to commodity/utility. It reveals how components naturally evolve through supply and demand competition, making dependencies, leverage points, and strategic options visible.

Wardley's core argument about digital sovereignty is that most sovereignty conversations happen without maps — they are storytelling exercises disconnected from the actual landscape. His "Sovereignty and Landscape" analysis argues that sovereignty, like physical territory, requires cartographic understanding before strategic decisions can be made.

In this paper, Wardley Mapping is applied to: map the digital dependency landscape of a bioregional entity; identify which components belong in Self, Together, or Others categories; and anticipate how the landscape is evolving, where new leverage points are emerging.

Igor Calzada's research, spanning from "Smart City Citizenship" (2021) through his recent work on data cooperatives and algorithmic nations, provides a multi-layered analytical framework for understanding digital sovereignty:

  • Institutional sovereignty — embedded in public agencies, hospitals, municipalities. The capacity of existing institutions to govern their own digital infrastructure.
  • Cooperative sovereignty — realized through federated data cooperatives and trusts. New institutional forms enabling collective data governance.
  • Geopolitical sovereignty — enacted through interregional alliances and AI standards. The macro-level context shaping what local sovereignty is possible.

Calzada's concept of "Algorithmic Nations" — drawing on Arendtian political theory — demonstrates how communities can attain self-determination through data sovereignty, offering a post-Westphalian frame particularly relevant for bioregional governance.

Stance

Epistemological Commitments

This is a living analysis, not a final report. It is produced through an action research methodology — learning by doing, reflecting, and iterating. The sensemaking process itself (design sprints, Wardley mapping workshops, stakeholder co-creation sessions, meeting dialogues) is not merely how this document was produced; it is an integral part of what the document advocates.

We acknowledge the boundaries between solid knowledge, reasoned speculation, and open questions. The content status indicators (Draft, In Progress, Outline) are not bugs — they are features of a document that takes its own living nature seriously.

We are oriented by systems thinking — attending to feedback loops, emergence, unintended consequences, and the irreducibility of complex adaptive systems to simple causal models. The regenerative digital ecosystem is not a machine to be engineered but an ecology to be cultivated.
