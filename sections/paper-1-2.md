Sovereignty is fundamentally different from independence. It is inherently relational, not isolationist. Medieval guilds understood something we have forgotten: mastery compounds through community, not competition. A guild member was sovereign not because they worked alone, but because they belonged to a network of mutual obligation, shared standards, and collective capability that no single patron could destroy.

The Dutch government's digital stack model and Ton Zijlstra's framework reveal that the strategic question is not "do everything yourself" — it is a three-way choice:

- **Self** — Layers core to organizational identity where dependency creates existential risk.
- **Together** — Layers with shared requirements, where collective capability exceeds individual capacity.
- **Others** — Layers sufficiently commoditized where vendor competition keeps quality high.

The sovereignty movement's purpose is expanding the "Together" category — building collective capability where dependency creates vulnerability but individual action is inefficient.

Igor Calzada's research identifies three overlapping layers through which sovereignty can be enacted: *institutional sovereignty*, embedded in public agencies and organizations; *cooperative sovereignty*, realized through federated data cooperatives and trusts; and *geopolitical sovereignty*, enacted through interregional alliances and standards. This multi-layered framing moves beyond the binary of "sovereign or dependent" toward a nuanced architecture of graduated autonomy.

We therefore prefer the framing of **digital self-determination** — the capacity of individuals, organizations, and communities to make meaningful choices about their digital infrastructure, data, and economic participation. Self-determination implies agency within relationship, not isolation.
