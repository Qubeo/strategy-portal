There is a moment in every dependency crisis when an organization realizes it has been building on someone else's land. The cloud provider changes terms. The software vendor pivots. The critical service gets acquired. Choices made in procurement meetings years ago become existential threats.

This is the sovereignty moment — when abstraction becomes concrete, when "strategic autonomy" shifts from policy document to operational imperative. Across Europe, municipalities, SMEs, and public institutions are confronting a landscape of digital dependencies that was assembled incrementally but now constitutes a structural vulnerability.

Yet most conversations about digital sovereignty happen, as Simon Wardley observes, *without maps*. They are storytelling exercises disconnected from the actual landscape of dependencies and evolution — equivalent to generals trying to win a war by giving it the right name. Without situational awareness of what we depend on, how those dependencies are evolving, and where our leverage actually lies, sovereignty remains an aspiration rather than a strategy.

The deeper problem is that sovereignty, even when pursued with maps and strategic clarity, requires an economic paradigm to inhabit. Sovereignty *from* extractive platforms is necessary — but sovereignty *for what*? Without answering this question, digital sovereignty risks becoming a protectionist reflex rather than a generative project.
